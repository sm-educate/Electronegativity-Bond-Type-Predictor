Clear Concept Study Guide

Bonding Mechanism & Properties

Polar Covalent

1. Why This Bond Forms

Hydrogen (EN 2.20) and Chlorine (EN 3.16) are both non-metals seeking a stable noble gas configuration. Their electronegativity difference (ΔEN = 0.96) is not large enough for complete electron transfer, so they share a pair of electrons unequally.

2. Valence Electron Behavior

The shared electron pair spends more time closer to the more electronegative Chlorine nucleus, giving Cl a partial negative charge (δ−) and H a partial positive charge (δ+).

3. Expected Physical Properties

Melting & Boiling Points Generally low to moderate (unless giant covalent network); stronger dipole–dipole forces than non-polar molecules of similar mass.
Electrical Conductivity Does not conduct electricity in pure solid or liquid states (no free ions), though polar acids like HCl ionize in aqueous solution.
Solubility ("Like Dissolves Like") Generally soluble in polar solvents such as water due to dipole–dipole or hydrogen-bonding interactions.
FBISE & IGCSE Exam Golden Rule & Exceptions
  • Periodic Trend: Electronegativity increases across a period (left → right) and decreases down a group. Fluorine (3.98) is the most electronegative element.
  • HF Exception (ΔEN = 1.78): Even though ΔEN > 1.7, Hydrogen Fluoride is a polar covalent molecule with strong hydrogen bonding because H cannot lose its only electron to form a bare H+ ion in isolation.
  • AlCl3 & BeCl2 Covalent Character: Small, high-charge cations (Al3+, Be2+) polarize large Cl− electron clouds (Fajans' Rules), giving anhydrous AlCl3 (ΔEN = 1.55) and BeCl2 (ΔEN = 1.59) predominantly covalent character.
